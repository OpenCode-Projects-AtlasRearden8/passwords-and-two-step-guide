# 2. Strong Passphrases You Can Actually Remember

You will remember exactly one important password: the one that opens your
password manager. This chapter is about choosing that one well — long enough
to be strong, memorable enough to survive a bad week, and uniquely yours.

## Length beats cleverness

A password's real-world strength comes mostly from its length. Four common
words glued together beat a short password full of symbols, both for attackers
and for your memory. The classic demonstration:

- `Tr0ub4dor&3` — tortured, twelve-ish characters, hard to type, and the
  patterns are guessable.
- `harp-melon-tango-pillow` — four unrelated common words. Long, easy to type
  on a phone, and vastly harder to brute-force.

Long and boring is a fortress. Short and clever is a lock on a screen door.

## The recipe

1. **Pick four words** at random, not as one sensible sentence. Randomness is
   the secret ingredient — "correct horse battery staple" holds together
   because nothing links the words.
2. **Glue them with dashes** (or anything consistent) so they're easy to type.
3. **OPTIONAL: a personal flavor** — a number or word only you'd connect to,
   added in a spot you'll never forget. The passphrase must stay yours
   *without* this step; the flavor is garnish, not the foundation.
4. **Never publish yours.** Examples in guides (like the ones above!) are
   burned — strangers have tried them. Roll your own dice.

### Two ways to get real randomness

- **A random-word tool** (many password managers generate phrases; search for
  "random passphrase generator"). Have the tool produce one word at a time and
  write them down, or generate four-at-once and evaluate before committing.
- **Paper and dice the analog way.** For a strictly offline feel: write your
  own short word list (10–12 words on a page), roll a die to pick positions,
  and let chance do the choosing. Any honest random source works.

## Saying it with pictures (the memory part)

Humans remember **scenes**, not strings. Take your four words and force them
into one absurd movie you can replay:

`harp-melon-tango-pillow` becomes: *a melon in sneakers playing a glittering
harp on a staircase made of pillows, while a penguin judge calls out tango
steps.* Absurd is good — the brain can't help replaying weird scenes.

Say the sentence once a day for a week; type the passphrase once a day for a
week (in a private note you delete, or into the manager's unlock screen). By
day seven it's a reflex. This is the entire "training plan," and it fits into
commutes and bathroom queues.

## Where to keep the crutch while training

Write the passphrase on paper. Yes, on paper — while it's the only strong thing
you have:

- Keep the paper **home, in a drawer only you open**, not in a wallet or bag.
- Don't photograph it, don't save it as a phone note, don't text it to anyone.
- After a few weeks of daily use, shred it. Its training purpose is over —
  and by then you'll also have your rescue path from
  [chapter 4](04-backup-codes.md).

## Common traps to sidestep

- **Sentence-shaped passphrases.** Famous quotes and song lines live in word
  lists attackers already use. Random beats rhyming.
- **The plus-one master password.** Adding `!` to last year's password while
  keeping the same base word just broadcasts the pattern. Start from truly
  random words instead.
- **"AI-inspired" structure in a string.** Any pattern you can describe in one
  breath ("capitals at the ends, doubled letters, year at the end") is a
  pattern for someone else too.
- **Anchoring identity.** Your initials, kids' names, old street, pet — these
  are breached and social-media-guessed constantly. Keep people and places out.

## A sanity test

Applying checks shouldn't be heavy. Before committing, your passphrase has:

- [ ] at least four unrelated common words (15+ characters),
- [ ] no names, years, song lyrics, or catchphrases,
- [ ] a glue style you'll type the same way every day,
- [ ] survived being written down, then performed as a silly scene,
- [ ] and it is *not* shown to anyone, including your AI tool.

Good. That's the single strongest password in hour of effort per half-decade.
Now go make the vault breathe easier.
