# 4. Backup Codes You Can Actually Find Later

When you turn on two-step codes, almost every site hands you a short list of
one-time rescue codes — usually ten single-use strings like `7F3K-92QX`. These
are the fire-escape for your account: the way in when your phone is dead,
lost, at the bottom of a lake, or swapped away from you. Saving them isn't a
bonus step. **It is the step.**

## The two-minute habit

When the confirmation finishes, the site usually shows a page titled
*Backup codes*, *Recovery codes*, or *Scratch codes*, with a **Download**,
**Copy**, or **Print** button. The habit:

- [ ] Download or copy the codes **on the spot**, while you're still on that
      page. Don't trust your future self to come back.
- [ ] Put them somewhere you'll remember — the [storage spots](#storage-spots-that-survive-real-life) below.
- [ ] Do one dry run: sign out, sign in using a backup code as the second
      step, watch the door actually open. Then it's real to you, not an idea.

If you forget to save codes in the moment, no harm — the site always has a
*Regenerate* / *View* option under the security settings. But do plan on
getting them, because a rescue route you never took is just a rumor.

## Storage spots that survive real life

The right spot is one you can find in a bad moment and a stranger can't.
Different people, different answers:

- **Printed, in a folder you actually have.** A manila folder labeled with
  something you'd honestly search for ("*House: keys, codes, taxes*"). Paper
  beats cleverness: no battery, no lockout, no phishing.
- **One vault that isn't your password manager.** A photo in a private
  (locked) camera roll on a backup device, an offline notes app with a
  passphrase, a USB drive in the same folder as the insurance papers. Extra
  credit: the paper copy of [the passphrases chapter](02-strong-memorable-passphrases.md)
  and these live in the same real folder.
- **Inside the password manager**, as a secure note — good but not ideal: your
  manager already lives behind the passphrase and the codes are the thing you
  need when you *can't* remember it. Keep one copy *outside* it too if any
  account matters a lot.

What doesn't work, gently: a screenshot living in a camera roll with no label,
a note titled "codes" deep inside a cloud app you rarely open, anything that
depends on the same phone that's underwater.

Also decide who *else* may ever see them. If a spouse or sibling is your
emergency contact, then store one paper set where they can reach it — or
better, set up trusted-person access properly in
[chapter 7](07-sharing-access-with-a-trusted-person.md). A random coworker
should *never* be able to reach your codes.

## Not all accounts provide backup codes (and what to do instead)

- Big accounts (Google, Apple, Amazon, most banks) generally do give you a
  rescue path — codes, printable cards, or recovery phone numbers. Take it.
- Some smaller sites *also* offer **security questions**. Prefer the "the
  answers are impersonal, recordable, and not publicly checkable" route:
  treat answers as extra passwords (eg "favorite food" = "octopus garden in
  Delta") and save *those* answers with your backup codes, written down.
- A few sites offer only **Recovery via email**. That means your **email
  account's** own 2FA (and *its* backup codes) quietly becomes the keystone of
  everything. That's why [chapter 3](03-two-step-codes.md) says do email first.
- Hardware/security-key setups (like [passkeys](05-passkeys-plain-english.md)
  done carefully) usually must have their own spare registered — a second
  key, or a second passkey on another device — *in place of* backup codes.
  That's discussed in the [tidy plan](08-one-evening-account-tidy.md).

## Two habits that prevent heartbreak

- **Refresh after life changes:** new phone? Re-scan codes, and regenerate
  backups. New year: glance at the set and check they're not stale.
- **Regenerate if codes may have leaked:** a printing shop, a photo shared by
  accident (you can also just delete the photo), a computer unknown, a
  co-worker's desk. Regeneration makes old codes invalid everywhere, instantly,
  safely.

Backup codes done this way turn the "phone died" story from a crisis into a
ten-minute errand with a folder labeled *House: keys, codes, taxes*.
